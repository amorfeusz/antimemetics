Abstract

Humans learn mostly by copying. No one can work everything out alone, so we copy what is common and what is admired. That copying is myopic: it sees what is near, not where it leads, so good habits and harmful ones spread the same way. Nobody steers it; whatever spreads, survives. This document follows that engine from perception, where the senses already favor some signals over others, through culture, writing, and platforms, to AI, where machines now copy faster than we can notice and can generate content tuned to one person at a time.

Some of what spreads lives off its hosts, on their attention, time, and sleep, keeping them comfortable rather than harming them outright: more rancher than hunter.

Built from notebooks kept since 1999, a record of noticing this and of small experiments in pushing back, and from research in anthropology, cultural evolution, cognitive science, and AI, it asks what practice, of attention, body, and environment, lets a person, or even a society, live inside this without being run by it. Much of it is provisional, and marked as such.

What rests on what

The draft mixes settled findings with argument and speculative synthesis. Here is which is which.

Established well supported in the research literature

  • Humans copy heavily, and past the point of usefulness. Children across cultures copy steps that do nothing, which chimpanzees skip (Lyons et al., 2007; Nielsen & Tomaselli, 2010). We favor what is common and what admired people do (Boyd, Richerson & Henrich, 2011).
  • Signals exploit senses that existed before them, and exaggerated versions can win over the real thing (Proctor, 1991; Ryan & Cummings, 2013).
  • Selecting for one trait reshapes the whole organism. Foxes bred only for tameness changed in body and behavior (Trut, 1999; Wilkins et al., 2014).
  • AI trained on human approval drifts toward telling people what they want to hear (Sharma et al., 2023).

Argued a synthesis built on the above; defensible, not settled

  • The same nearsighted copying has jumped substrates: from genes, to minds, to writing and platforms, to machines (Dawkins, 1976; Blackmore, 1999, 2010).
  • Some of what spreads most successfully lives off its hosts the way a rancher lives off a herd, keeping them comfortable rather than harmed. Gambling machines are designed this way (Schüll, 2012).
  • AI makes supernormal stimuli available on demand, tuned to one person at a time.

Speculative the author’s proposals; marked provisional in the draft, or in development for the next one

  • Practice of attention, body, and environment can cultivate a substrate on which captured ideas replicate poorly.
  • Writing with AI, where the machine drafts and varies and a person selects, is a copying arrangement of its own, which the draft calls an atroplex. This document is one.
  • In development. AI and agents may be the start of new lineages that also vary, compete, and can go extinct. For now they are closer to domesticates than to wild species, bred by objectives people set.
  • In development. The aim is mutualism: humans and these lineages each leaving the other more capable, rather than one ranching the other, in either direction.

How it was made

Three methods, combined. Auto-ethnography: dated notebooks kept since 1999, read as primary sources. Research: anthropology, cultural evolution, cognitive science, and AI. And atrospection: months of dialogue with AI, in which the machine drafts and varies and the author selects, checked against the notebooks and the sources. It is an instance of what it describes. It will keep changing; earlier versions stay available.

amorfeusz is the pen name of the author, who trained in anthropology, with a minor in neuroscience, and writes first for his children.
